Current Research

  • The Threat of Faith: The Determinants and Legacies of Soviet Anti-Religious Repression draft available on request Religion has rarely faced state power as total as the Soviet Union's. Atheism was official policy for seven decades, and the state closed or destroyed 84 percent of the Orthodox churches standing in Russia in 1917. The campaign was nonetheless selective, and this paper asks what governed it and what it left behind. The evidence is a new dataset covering the near-universe of those churches – 21,238 buildings – with closure, destruction, and reopening dates, materials, and types recovered from free-text histories by a large language model. Even an unconditional ideological aim, the results show, was carried out like a constrained optimization. Facing limited enforcement capacity, the state acted first on the churches it most valued suppressing: older and larger buildings, those near past unrest, and those in places where the security apparatus was strong and the local church stock too sparse to strain it. Historical religiosity accounts for only part of the pattern. Conditional on acting, the disposal decision tracked reuse value: prominent masonry churches were closed and converted to secular use, while wooden and dilapidated ones were demolished, most sharply in the Stalin years. Using plausibly exogenous variation in repression built from these determinants, mainly the physical composition of the local 1917 stock, the paper estimates the long-run effect on religiosity today and finds it faint. Repression is associated with somewhat lower service attendance and a lower stated importance of religion, by 0.1 to 0.3 of a standard deviation, but with no measurable change in religious affiliation, identity, or belief. A cohort analysis indicates that these near-null local effects are most consistent with an impact of repression that operated at the national and generational level rather than locally, and with a partial recovery of religiosity since the Soviet collapse.
  • Equilibrium Behavior in Sequential Games: Evidence from Formula 1 with Dmitry Dagaev and Gleb Vasiliev — under review We study equilibrium behavior in a naturally occurring multi-period sequential game. Using detailed telemetry data from Formula 1 races, we analyze strategic pit-stop interactions between closely competing drivers, who repeatedly decide whether to act or wait while anticipating their rival's response. We first estimate how the timing of both drivers' decisions affects the probability of overtaking and use these estimates to characterize the payoffs of the sequential game, optimal responses, and equilibrium behavior. We then compare the model's predictions with observed decisions. Drivers respond systematically to their rivals' actions, and their responses broadly follow the comparative-static patterns implied by the model. However, we find substantial heterogeneity across teams: some exhibit response patterns considerably closer to the model-implied best responses than others, and closer adherence to these responses is associated with greater success in strategic interactions. Our results provide field evidence on equilibrium behavior in a sequential game in which the same agents make repeated, state-dependent decisions. [PDF]

Pre-Ph.D. research

  • Economics of Superstars under Hard Salary Cap: A Study of the National Hockey League unpublished undergraduate thesis The superstars who can boost the performance of their team, at the same time, can increase the inequality that may mitigate the positive effect of superstars and even make hiring them unfavorable. In this thesis, I model this trade-off by focusing on the National Hockey League (NHL) teams that operate under a hard salary cap. I estimate the parameters of the model using the data on the salaries of the NHL players, and the model suggests that teams should hire superstars under these conditions. I further study the potential heterogeneity of the effects and find that top players are less vulnerable to adverse inequality effects and benefit from the presence of superstars more than other players. Moreover, superstars may slightly decrease the performance of their peers who compete with them for time on ice, but the effect on players with other roles is strictly positive. Based on these results, NHL teams should hire superstars but avoid creating competition among them. [PDF]
